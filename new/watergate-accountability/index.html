Story 10 • 1972–1974

Presentation experiment: Evidence file

Watergate Accountability

No President Above the Law

Investigators, courts, Congress, and the public prove that no president stands beyond the reach of law.

Begin the story ↓

Scene 01

A piece of tape

Shortly after midnight on June 17, 1972, security guard Frank Wills noticed tape covering a door latch at the Watergate office complex in Washington, D.C. He removed it. When he returned and found the latch taped again, he called police.

Officers arrested five men inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters carrying cameras, cash, and electronic surveillance equipment. The burglars had connections to President Richard Nixon's reelection organization. Yet the break-in itself was only the opening act of Watergate.

The greater constitutional danger came from the effort to prevent investigators and voters from learning what had happened. Money was used to buy silence. Officials gave misleading statements. The Central Intelligence Agency was asked to interfere with the Federal Bureau of Investigation's inquiry by falsely invoking national security. The machinery of the presidency was turned toward protecting the president politically.

Nixon won reelection in November 1972 by a landslide. That victory did not erase possible crimes. Democracy is not a system in which winning an election gives officials permission to misuse government power. Elections grant authority for a term; they do not grant immunity from law.

Wills did not know he was beginning a national crisis. He simply noticed something wrong, checked it, and reported it. Accountability often starts with an ordinary person performing an ordinary duty carefully.

Scene 02

The cover-up starts to crack

No single institution uncovered Watergate alone. The FBI traced evidence. Federal prosecutors brought charges. Judge John Sirica pressed the convicted burglars to reveal whether others were involved. Reporters Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein connected the break-in to Nixon's campaign and kept unanswered questions in public view.

In February 1973, the Senate voted unanimously to create a bipartisan Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities. Its televised hearings allowed millions of Americans to watch testimony and judge contradictions for themselves. Former White House counsel John Dean described a cover-up reaching into the administration.

Then former presidential aide Alexander Butterfield revealed that Nixon had installed a voice-activated recording system in the White House. The tapes could show whether Dean was truthful and what the president had said in private. They transformed the investigation from a contest of memories into a fight over evidence.

Nixon argued that executive privilege and separation of powers allowed him to withhold presidential conversations. Confidential advice is important to governing, but investigators argued that privilege could not become a shield for evidence of crime. The Senate committee and the Watergate special prosecutor issued subpoenas.

The system worked slowly and imperfectly. Witnesses lied. Officials resisted. Reporters made errors and corrected them. Partisans disagreed. Accountability emerged because separate institutions continued checking one another rather than accepting the White House's account as final.

Scene 03

Law reaches the White House

Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox demanded relevant tapes. On October 20, 1973, Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire him. Richardson refused and resigned. Deputy Attorney General William Ruckelshaus also refused and left office. Solicitor General Robert Bork, then acting attorney general, carried out the order. The episode became known as the Saturday Night Massacre.

The firing did not end the investigation. Public outrage intensified it. Leon Jaworski became special prosecutor and continued seeking evidence. The House Judiciary Committee began an impeachment inquiry.

The conflict reached the Supreme Court in *United States v. Nixon*. On July 24, 1974, the Court ruled unanimously that a generalized claim of presidential confidentiality could not defeat the demonstrated need for evidence in a criminal trial. The president had to obey the subpoena.

This did not abolish executive privilege. It established that privilege was limited and could not place the president beyond judicial process. The Constitution's branches had different powers: Congress could investigate and impeach, courts could decide legal disputes, prosecutors could pursue criminal evidence, and the executive could not define the boundaries of its own accountability without challenge.

When the tapes were released, one recording from June 23, 1972 showed Nixon approving a plan to use the CIA to impede the FBI investigation. The evidence directly contradicted his public denials.

Scene 04

A president resigns

The House Judiciary Committee approved three articles of impeachment: obstruction of justice, abuse of power, and contempt of Congress. After the decisive tape became public, senior Republican members told Nixon that his remaining support in Congress had collapsed.

On August 8, Nixon announced that he would resign. The next morning he signed a brief letter, and the resignation became effective when Secretary of State Henry Kissinger initialed it at 11:35. Vice President Gerald Ford took the presidential oath.

Nixon was not removed by a coup, an angry crowd, or a military command. Constitutional pressure made continued office impossible. Courts issued orders. Congress prepared to vote. Evidence changed political support. The presidency transferred peacefully according to law.

Ford pardoned Nixon a month later, a decision that remains controversial. Several former officials were convicted, while reforms strengthened campaign-finance rules, ethics disclosure, public access to records, and congressional oversight. No statute could guarantee honest government. Watergate also damaged trust and encouraged a cynical belief that all public service is corrupt.

The better lesson is more demanding: institutions restrain abuse only when people inside and outside them do their jobs. Wills called police. Investigators followed money. witnesses testified. Journalists kept asking. Judges enforced subpoenas. Members of both parties placed evidence above loyalty to one leader.

Scene 05

Why this is the Spirit of 1776

The Revolution rejected the idea that a ruler's authority placed him above the people and their law. The Constitution replaced a king with an elected president, but elections alone could not prevent executive power from becoming personal power.

Watergate demonstrated the Spirit of 1776 because the country held its highest elected official answerable to institutions he did not control. The principle was not that presidents must be powerless or that political opponents are always right. It was that claims of authority require legal limits, evidence, and review.

Accountability is not disloyalty to the nation. It is loyalty to a republic in which office belongs temporarily to a person but permanently to the public. Watergate's lasting achievement was not scandal. It was proof that even a president who had won overwhelmingly could be forced to answer when evidence showed that he had abused his trust.
