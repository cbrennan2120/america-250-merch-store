September 11, 2001 · United Airlines Flight 93

They learned.
They voted.
They acted.

In the final minutes of Flight 93, ordinary passengers and crew gathered what they could learn, made a collective decision, and moved together to stop a larger attack.

Read what happened Explore the Spirit of 1776

Forty passengers and crew were aboard Flight 93. This page remembers their shared courage without recreating the violence of their final moments.

A modern echo

Resistance begins when ordinary people recognize that the decision belongs to them.

The people aboard Flight 93 were not reenacting 1776, and they were not soldiers waiting for commands. They were travelers and working crew members trapped inside an attack whose purpose they had to discover for themselves.

The parallel is narrower—and more powerful. Like citizens facing coercive power in earlier American moments, they shared information, weighed an impossible choice, and acted together. Their authority came from one another.

Interpretive scene: calls from the aircraft transformed scattered facts into a shared understanding.

01 · The calls

The truth reached the cabin one conversation at a time.

Flight 93 left Newark for San Francisco at 8:42 a.m. Four hijackers seized the aircraft at about 9:28. In the minutes that followed, passengers and crew used seatback Airfones and cell phones to contact relatives, operators, and authorities.

Thirteen people aboard made 37 calls. From those conversations they learned that hijacked aircraft had struck the World Trade Center and the Pentagon. What first looked like an isolated hijacking was something else: their aircraft was likely intended to become another weapon.

Information changed their choices. People in different rows compared what they knew. Calls associated with Tom Burnett, Todd Beamer, Jeremy Glick, Mark Bingham, Sandy Bradshaw, CeeCee Lyles, and others preserve only fragments of a much larger group effort.

Interpretive scene: the group’s reported vote made a private realization into a collective choice.

02 · The decision

They did not wait for rescue. They made a decision.

Phone accounts described passengers discussing a plan to rush the cockpit. Jeremy Glick told his wife that the group took a vote. Others ended calls to join the effort. The evidence does not give us a perfect transcript of the cabin, but it gives us something unmistakable: consultation became consent, and consent became action.

“They decided, and acted.”

— The 9/11 Commission Report

This was democracy stripped to its most basic form. No podium. No procedure manual. No promise of safety. Just people facing the same danger and choosing together what they were willing to do.

Interpretive scene: the counterattack is shown through movement and solidarity, without depicting combat or impact.

03 · The counterattack

The choice mattered because they moved together.

The counterattack began at 9:57 a.m. Recordings from the cockpit captured a sustained struggle as the hijackers rolled and pitched the aircraft. The passengers and crew kept pressing forward.

At 10:03, Flight 93 crashed near Shanksville, Pennsylvania. Everyone aboard was killed. The aircraft never reached Washington. Investigators concluded that the hijackers’ most likely target was the United States Capitol, where Congress was in session.

The people aboard could not save themselves. Their action nevertheless denied the attackers control over the ending they had planned—and protected unknown people on the ground.

Why this belongs in the Spirit of 1776

Civic courage is not a costume. It is a choice made under pressure.

The spirit of 1776 is often reduced to flags, famous names, and battlefield scenes. Its harder claim is that ordinary people possess political agency: power is not reserved for rulers, officers, or institutions. On Flight 93, passengers and crew used the freedom still available to them—the freedom to learn, deliberate, decide, and resist.

The circumstances were modern and singular. The civic principle was old: when power becomes coercion, people may discover that responsibility has arrived before permission.
