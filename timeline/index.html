1765–1783

The long Revolution

The United States did not appear in a single afternoon. Protest, war, alliance, and negotiation changed what independence could mean.

  1. 1765

    The Stamp Act

    Parliament taxed printed materials, intensifying arguments about representation and the limits of imperial power.

    Library of Congress source (opens in a new tab)
  2. 1770

    The Boston Massacre

    British soldiers fired into a Boston crowd. Competing accounts and Paul Revere’s influential print turned the event into powerful propaganda.

    Library of Congress source (opens in a new tab)
  3. 1773

    The Boston Tea Party

    Protesters destroyed East India Company tea, and Parliament answered with punitive laws that brought colonies closer together.

    National Park Service source (opens in a new tab)
  4. 1775

    Lexington and Concord

    Fighting began in Massachusetts on April 19, transforming years of political conflict into open war.

    National Park Service source (opens in a new tab)
  5. 1776

    Independence declared

    Congress adopted the Declaration on July 4. John Dunlap’s first printed copies began carrying the words outward the next day.

    National Archives source (opens in a new tab)
  6. 1777

    Saratoga

    An American victory in New York helped persuade France to enter the war as an ally, reshaping the conflict.

    National Park Service source (opens in a new tab)
  7. 1781

    Yorktown

    A combined American and French campaign trapped Cornwallis, ending the war’s last major battle.

    National Park Service source (opens in a new tab)
  8. 1783

    The Treaty of Paris

    The treaty formally ended the war and recognized the United States as free, sovereign, and independent.

    National Archives source (opens in a new tab)
