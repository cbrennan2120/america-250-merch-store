Story 07 / 1861–1865

Union Soldiers and Emancipation

Saving the Nation by Destroying Slavery

Enslaved people and Black soldiers helped transform a war for Union into a war that destroyed slavery.

Begin the story

Scene 01

Freedom reaches Union lines

The Civil War began in April 1861 after eleven slaveholding states attempted to leave the United States. Their new Confederacy was created to protect slavery. President Abraham Lincoln's first declared war aim, however, was preserving the Union, not immediately abolishing slavery everywhere.

Enslaved people helped change what the war would mean.

In May 1861, three men named Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory, and James Townsend escaped work on Confederate defenses and reached Union-held Fort Monroe in Virginia. Their enslaver demanded their return under the Fugitive Slave Act. General Benjamin Butler refused. Because Confederates treated enslaved labor as useful to their war effort, he reasoned that their claimed labor could be retained as contraband of war. The term soon spread, even though Butler did not use that exact phrase in his initial exchange.

The label was dehumanizing, but the decision created an opening. More enslaved people came to Union lines. They carried children, found relatives, supplied intelligence, worked for wages, and built new communities under punishing conditions. Federal policy remained uncertain, and camps suffered from hunger and disease. Yet each arrival made one fact harder to avoid: the Union army could not fight a slaveholders' rebellion while continually returning people to the slavery that sustained it.

Freedom did not first move from Washington outward. It also moved from plantations toward Union lines, carried by people who acted before the government had settled what to do.

Scene 02

The war changes

Congress moved in stages. Confiscation laws weakened slaveholders' claims and authorized freedom for some enslaved people connected to the rebellion. Lincoln also moved cautiously, concerned about loyal slave states, divided Northern opinion, constitutional limits, and the survival of the Union.

By 1862, military necessity and antislavery pressure were converging. Enslaved people were already undermining Confederate labor. Abolitionists demanded action. Black leaders argued that a nation asking men to die for the Union could not keep protecting human bondage.

After the Union stopped Robert E. Lee's invasion at Antietam, Lincoln issued the Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation on September 22, 1862. It warned that enslaved people in states still rebelling on January 1 would be declared free. On that date, Lincoln signed the final proclamation.

The document did not abolish slavery throughout the nation. It applied to areas in rebellion, not loyal slave states or Confederate areas already under specified Union control. Freedom on paper also depended on Union military success. But the proclamation transformed the official purpose and conduct of the war. Wherever Union power advanced into covered territory, it carried an order of emancipation. Foreign governments now had greater difficulty recognizing a Confederacy openly fighting to preserve slavery.

The proclamation also authorized Black men to join United States forces. A war originally announced as a struggle only to restore the Union now officially joined Union and freedom.

Scene 03

The liberated become liberators

Black Americans had sought permission to serve from the war's beginning. After federal policy changed, recruitment accelerated. By the end of the war, roughly 180,000 Black soldiers had served in the United States Army, with additional Black sailors in the Navy. Many had escaped slavery themselves. Others were free men from the North. Their families and communities supported recruitment while confronting racist violence and unequal treatment.

The United States Colored Troops usually served in segregated units commanded by white officers. Black soldiers initially received lower pay, faced limited promotion, and risked murder or re-enslavement if captured by Confederates. They protested unequal pay and proved their commitment in garrison duty, labor, raids, and battle.

The 54th Massachusetts Infantry became one of the best-known Black regiments. On July 18, 1863, it led an assault on Fort Wagner near Charleston, South Carolina. The attack failed and caused terrible losses, including the death of Colonel Robert Gould Shaw. But the regiment's discipline and courage challenged racist claims that Black men would not fight. Recruitment grew.

Military service did not make emancipation a gift from the army. It made Black Americans armed participants in destroying slavery. They guarded supply lines, liberated communities, fought Confederate troops, and made it politically and militarily harder for the United States to retreat from freedom.

Scene 04

Freedom advances with the army

Union armies became engines of emancipation as they occupied Confederate territory. Their record was not uniformly noble: soldiers could be racist, exploitative, or hostile, and not every Union soldier fought from abolitionist conviction. Some enlisted to preserve the nation, defend their communities, or earn wages. Their motives varied.

Their victory nevertheless became essential to slavery's destruction. At Appomattox in April 1865, Black soldiers of the United States Colored Troops were among the forces that helped block Confederate escape routes and witnessed surrender. Across the South, Union success gave force to emancipation while freed people reunited families, negotiated labor, founded schools and churches, served in government, and pursued citizenship.

The proclamation rested on presidential war powers and left slavery legal in places it did not cover. A permanent constitutional end required the Thirteenth Amendment. Congress passed it on January 31, 1865, and the states ratified it on December 6. It abolished slavery and involuntary servitude in the United States, except as punishment for a crime.

That exception would have serious consequences, and emancipation did not deliver equality, safety, land, or voting rights by itself. Freedom opened a new struggle over what citizenship would mean.

Scene 05

Why this is the Spirit of 1776

The Declaration of Independence announced universal rights while the nation tolerated human bondage. The Civil War forced the United States to choose whether its founding promise could survive alongside a system that denied personhood to millions.

The Spirit of 1776 appears here not in a simple portrait of a president freeing passive subjects. It appears in enslaved people walking to Union lines, Black citizens demanding the right to fight, soldiers accepting sacrifice, lawmakers turning wartime freedom into constitutional law, and a nation changing its purpose under pressure from those it had excluded.

Union victory preserved republican government against a rebellion built to protect slavery. Emancipation made that victory morally larger: the nation could survive only by moving closer to the principle that liberty belongs to people, not rulers or enslavers. The work remained unfinished, but slavery's legal destruction made every later struggle for equal citizenship possible.
