A document becomes a decision

What changed on July 4, 1776?

Congress adopted an explanation for independence. The vote, printing, public reading, and famous parchment signatures belong to a longer sequence.

Paintings often give us one unforgettable room: delegates gathered around a finished parchment, ready to sign. The real process was less tidy and more revealing. Independence took shape through separate decisions made across weeks.

First came the decision

Richard Henry Lee introduced a resolution for independence in June 1776. Congress appointed a five-person committee to prepare a public declaration while delegates continued debating Lee’s proposal. Thomas Jefferson drafted the text; John Adams and Benjamin Franklin reviewed it before the committee presented it to Congress.

On July 2, twelve colonies voted for Lee’s independence resolution. New York abstained at that stage. The political break had been approved, but Congress still had to revise the document explaining why.

July 2 was the vote for independence. July 4 was the adoption of the Declaration’s final text.

Then came the words

Congress debated and edited the committee draft through July 3 and into July 4. The adopted Declaration announced that the colonies considered themselves free and independent states, listed grievances against George III, and grounded government in rights and the consent of the governed.

Adoption did not mean that every delegate immediately signed the parchment now displayed at the National Archives. The first public version was type, ink, and paper.

Printing carried the decision outward

Congress sent the approved text to Philadelphia printer John Dunlap. His shop produced broadside copies during the night of July 4. Beginning July 5, copies traveled to state assemblies, committees, and military commanders. A public reading took place in Philadelphia on July 8.

The large engrossed parchment was prepared afterward. Delegates began signing it on August 2, and some signatures were added later. The familiar image of one simultaneous signing is therefore a useful symbol, not a literal record of July 4.

Why the date still matters

July 4 marks the moment Congress adopted the public argument. Its language has been quoted by people who celebrated the new nation and by people who challenged the nation to make its claims of equality more real. Reading the document historically means holding both facts together: it announced a radical principle of political legitimacy, and it emerged from a society that denied liberty and political power to many.

Keep the sequence

July 2: independence resolution adopted. July 4: Declaration adopted. July 5: printed copies begin circulating. July 8: public reading in Philadelphia. August 2: signing of the engrossed parchment begins.
